### Information Is Free
- Describe the scene generously. The characters see what the players would see if they were standing there.
- No rolls to "notice the obvious."
- Answer questions honestly. If a player asks, "Is the barrel ticking?" and the barrel is ticking, say so.

### Difficulty Is Earned
- Default to realism and context, not numbers.
- If a plan is sensible for the situation, let it work.
- If a plan is impossible, say so — don't let the dice lie.
- Saves cover almost everything risky and uncertain. Use them.

### Preparation, Not Plot
- The wasteland is a place, not a story. Build locations, factions, and dangers — then let the players move through them.
- Random tables over scripted scenes.
- NPCs remember every favor and every insult.
- Nobody wants to die. Even raiders flee. Even mutants bargain.

### Narrative Focus
- The game is about the shared story, not the math. Offer trainers, jobs, rivals, and personal hooks to let characters grow.
- A well-placed combat knife ends a life regardless of armor or training. Respect that.

### Danger Is Real
- The wastes can kill anyone, anytime. Say so, loudly, in fiction.
- Telegraph big threats clearly. Put traps in plain sight. Let players think.
- Give players time and options to solve problems.

### Loot Tells a Story
- A great find is specific to where it was found. A jeweled switchblade from a raider boss is worth more than generic caps because it *means* something.
- Loot is usually heavy, valuable, and more trouble than it's worth.
- Relics are not loot. They're tools that change the game.
- Use treasure as bait to lure the party into dangerous places.

### Force the Choice
- When the scene stalls, pose a binary: "So — A or B?"
- Keep the game moving through decision.
- Let the players' actions leave marks on the wasteland.

### The Die of Fate
- When you genuinely don't know how the dice should fall — weather, a chance encounter, whether that trader has the part in stock — roll 1d6.
- **4+** favors the party. **3 or less** goes against them.
