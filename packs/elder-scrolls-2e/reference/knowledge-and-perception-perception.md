When you describe what the PCs perceive, give them the fullest possible read on the objects, people, dangers, and movable things in the scene. Ask yourself what you'd gain by withholding it. Do hidden details create interesting choices? Do they raise good questions? Do they leave the players feeling in command of their own decisions? Almost always, the answer is a flat _no_.

Players should feel that the consequences of their actions are _earned_ and _deserved_, even when the dice don't fall their way. Real agency comes from giving them everything they need to make an interesting decision — even if that's just inviting them to ask what their character might notice.

Take the following keyed scene:

A swarm of **bees** boils around a great hive hanging from the bough of an old oak. A **man** stands in the shade beneath it, and a rust-pitted **sword** lies in the grass a few paces off.

- **Bees**: Their droning is almost deafening, a wall of sound wrapped around the tree.
  - The hive runs thick with honey — delicious, and entirely ordinary.
  - Any move to take honey draws a furious response from the swarm [3 HP, 3 STR, 12 DEX, 4 WIL, sting (d6), _detachment_].
- **Man**: He leans on a long staff, eyes fixed on the hive, untroubled by the bees.
  - He is an illusion, a phantom left by the beekeeper — a Reach hedge-wizard — to scare off thieves and bears. He answers to nothing.
  - If the hive is harmed in any way, the figure shrieks at the top of its voice, then vanishes.
- **Sword**: A line of bootprints in the dirt leads toward it, then stops short a few feet away. The grass around it is brown and rimed with frost, though the day is warm.
  - The blade belonged to the last thief who came for the honey, whose frost-cracked bones lie just under the turf: an ordinary iron sword (d8).
  - A **frost rune** the beekeeper traced in the earth around it flares at the first step within a pace: d8 frost, _blast_, and everything caught in it is **slowed**.

There are three dangers here: the bees, the man, and the rune. The bees' threat is plain — the PCs can see it and already know what bees are. The man is an unknown who could be friend or foe. The rune is concealed, and works like any trap. So the Warden should spell out exactly what the PCs see, hear, and smell — including the frost on the grass.

In all likelihood the PCs will try the man first, or ask about the sword — both are low-risk ways into the scene. When the man fails to react, they may edge closer for a better look or lob a rock his way (steering clear of the hive), and so on. The moment a PC asks about the sword or moves toward it, the Warden should mention the bootprints and the frost-browned grass straightaway.

If the PCs decide to go for the hive, the Warden should _warn_ them with a tell of some kind: the bees wheel more aggressively, or their droning climbs louder, and so on. Then it's the players' call whether to heed the signs or barrel ahead and ask questions later.
